Quick reference

Level measureSaltSodium

Sodium is 39.3% of salt by mass (22.99 / 58.44). 1 g salt = 393 mg sodium = 17.1 mmol. Metric teaspoon = 5 mL. Australian tablespoon = 20 mL, not 15 mL. Spoon figures assume level, unpacked measures and can be out by 10 to 20%. For amounts under 1 g, a 0.01 g jewellery scale is far more reliable than a spoon or a 1 g kitchen scale.

Method

  1. Combine sugar, water, salt and lemon juice in a small saucepan.
  2. Heat gently, stirring, until the sugar has fully dissolved. Do not let it colour.
  3. Simmer for 2 minutes. The citric acid partially inverts the sucrose into free glucose and fructose, which is what stops the syrup crystallising back out as it cools.
  4. Cool completely to room temperature before filling. Hot syrup will deform a TPU soft flask.
  5. Transfer to a 150 mL wide-spout soft flask. Avoid bite valves, they clog at this viscosity.
  6. Keeps about 2 weeks refrigerated. If it still crystallises, swap 15 g of the sugar for golden syrup.

Note This is a concentrated gel, roughly 65 to 70% w/v. It is designed to be swallowed and chased with plain water, never sipped as a drink. Diluting it into a bottle produces a solution well above 8% carbohydrate, which slows gastric emptying and draws water into the gut lumen.

In-run fuelling and race protocol checklist

Timing

WhenAction
T−2.5 to 3 hrHigh-carbohydrate, low-fibre, low-fat meal. Nothing untested.

In runTake one third of a flask about 200 m before an aid station or fountain, never after, then chase with plain water.

Aid station technique

  • Pinch the paper cup rim closed into a narrow V-spout. This is the difference between ingesting 40 mL and 100 mL.
  • Expect 80 to 110 mL per cup with good technique, 120 to 150 mL if you walk through, 40 to 70 mL if you grab and drink at full pace.
  • Practise it once at a park tap before race day. It is the highest-yield skill on this page.
  • Confirm at the expo which stations carry plain water and which carry sports drink. Landing a high-concentration drink mix on top of your own gel is a bad afternoon, and race day is not the time to debut an untested product.

Sequencing rule

Concentrated carbohydrate sitting undiluted in the stomach is what produces cramping and nausea. Gel first, water within the next 200 m, every time.

Rehearsal

  • Run at least two long sessions with the exact flask, exact syrup, exact caffeine timing and exact start time you intend to race with.
  • Gut tolerance is trainable. Intestinal transporter expression responds to repeated in-run carbohydrate exposure over roughly 4 to 6 weeks, so start early and do it on every long run.
  • Nothing new in the final fortnight.
How these numbers are calculated
  • Duration = distance × pace.
  • Adjusted sweat rate = baseline × (1 + 0.025 × (temp − 18)), floored at 50% of baseline, then × 1.15 if the humidity or sun modifier is on.
  • Allowable deficit = body mass × 0.02. Performance decrement becomes consistent beyond roughly 2 to 3% body mass loss, so this is an envelope you are permitted to finish inside, not a volume you must drink up to.
  • Replacement need = sweat loss − allowable deficit, floored at zero. This is deliberately conservative. It ignores metabolic water production and water liberated from glycogen breakdown, which together contribute in the order of 100 to 200 mL per hour, so real-world need is usually a little lower than shown.
  • Absorption ceiling = 750 mL/hr. Gastric emptying of a low-carbohydrate fluid tops out around 600 to 1000 mL/hr and falls further with rising intensity and dehydration. The recommendation is capped here regardless of calculated need.
  • Carbohydrate: nil under 45 min; 15 to 30 g or a mouth rinse for 45 to 75 min; 30 to 60 g/hr beyond 75 min; 60 to 90 g/hr beyond 150 min, which requires a glucose and fructose blend to exceed the roughly 60 g/hr SGLT1 saturation point for glucose alone.
  • Sodium: not required under 90 min. Optional, up to 300 mg/hr, for 90 to 150 min. 300 to 600 mg/hr beyond 150 min, or earlier if estimated sweat loss reaches 2 L or it is 26°C or hotter. Sodium intake does not prevent exercise-associated hyponatraemia in the face of over-drinking. Sweat sodium concentration varies roughly fourfold between individuals and cannot be estimated from a calculator.

Estimates only. Individual sweat rate and sweat sodium vary widely and are best established by direct measurement. This tool is general information for healthy adults, not medical advice, and is not appropriate for anyone with cardiac, renal or endocrine disease, or taking diuretics, SGLT2 inhibitors or other agents affecting fluid and electrolyte balance.
